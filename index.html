Scenario 1 — Conventional

20% Down Payment

The straightforward baseline. Put 20% down, avoid PMI, lock a 30-year fixed rate. Predictable, widely available, and keeps a modest cash cushion.

Why this works
20% eliminates PMI, saving $200–400/month vs lower down scenarios. Fixed rate means your payment never changes regardless of what the Fed does. Most lenders offer it and qualifying is straightforward.
Watch out for
At $850K, 20% is $170K — nearly all your cash. After closing costs (~$17K) you'd have roughly $13K left. That's a thin emergency fund heading into homeownership with a baby on the way.
Good if...
Your wife returns to work within 12–18 months, you have savings not counted here, or you're comfortable running lean in year one. Simplest option to execute.
Real risk
Any major unexpected expense — HVAC, roof, medical — in year one hits while cash is nearly zero. New homeowners typically spend $5K–15K in the first year on things they didn't anticipate.

Assumes 6.5% fixed, 30-year term, 1.2% property tax, $180/mo homeowners insurance. Net income $8,800/mo. Available cash ~$200K.

Scenario 2 — Rate Buydown

20% Down + 1 Point

Same down payment as the baseline, but pay one point upfront (1% of loan) to buy the rate down ~0.25%. Costs more at closing but reduces your payment for the life of the loan.

Why this works
Lower payment for the full 30 years. If you stay 5+ years, the upfront cost pays off and every month after break-even is pure savings. Total interest paid over the life of the loan is meaningfully lower.
The break-even problem
One point saves ~$100–115/month but costs $6,800+ upfront. Break-even is year 4–5. If you refinance before then because rates drop, you lose that money entirely.
Good if...
You plan to stay 7–10+ years and don't expect to refinance soon. Works best when current rates are near a local peak.
Real risk
Leaves even less cash after closing than the baseline — roughly $6K. Almost no cushion for unexpected expenses in year one.

Assumes 6.25% rate after 1-point buydown. All other assumptions same as Scenario 1.

Scenario 3 — More Equity

25% Down Payment

Larger down payment means smaller loan and lower payment. Reaching 25% on an $850K home requires ~$212.5K — meaning the $50K retirement loan fills the gap between your cash and the target.

Why this works
Lowest monthly P&I of the conventional options. More equity from day one means hitting refinance thresholds sooner if rates drop. Some lenders offer slightly better rates at 25%+ LTV.
The retirement loan problem
Repaying a 401(k) loan uses after-tax dollars while losing years of compound growth. If you leave your job the balance may become due immediately — real risk with variable income and a growing family.
Watch out for
The retirement loan adds $400–600/month in repayment on top of the mortgage. You lower one payment but add another. Net relief is smaller than the headline number suggests.
Good if...
Your job is very stable, you're certain you won't leave in the next 5 years, and the lower mortgage payment materially changes your cash flow picture.

Scenario 4 — FHA Loan

FHA 3.5% Down

Because your wife hasn't owned a home in 3+ years, she qualifies as a first-time buyer under FHA rules — making you jointly eligible. Minimal down payment keeps most cash liquid, but mortgage insurance adds to the monthly cost.

⚠ FHA loan limits vary by county. Most high-cost metros allow up to ~$1.15M in 2026 — confirm your specific county before proceeding.

Why this works
Preserves ~$170K in cash after closing — a massive liquidity advantage heading into new homeownership and parenthood. Slightly lower rate than conventional. Easier to qualify with lower credit requirements.
The MIP trap
FHA mortgage insurance cannot be removed by reaching 20% equity. Unlike PMI, you're stuck with it until you refinance into a conventional loan ($3–6K in closing costs). Plan that refinance for year 3–5.
Watch out for
An upfront MIP fee (1.75% of loan) is added to your loan balance at closing, increasing total debt. Monthly MIP adds $300–400/mo on top of P&I, making total monthly cost higher than the 20% conventional despite the lower rate.
Best exit strategy
After 3–5 years of payments and home appreciation, refinance into a conventional loan once you've reached 20% equity. Drop the MIP and potentially capture a better rate if the market cooperates.

Scenario 5 — FHA + Lump Sum

FHA 3.5% + $150K Principal Payment

Start with the low FHA down payment to preserve cash and closing flexibility, then make a $150,000 lump-sum principal payment shortly after closing. This dramatically reduces the balance, total interest, and payoff timeline — while giving you liquidity during the critical transition period.

⚡ $150,000 principal payment applied at month 3
⚠ Confirm with your lender that there is no prepayment penalty on the FHA loan before closing. Most FHA loans have none, but verify.

The core insight
By keeping cash liquid at closing, you have full flexibility on timing. If you close in month 1 and drop $150K in month 3, you've only paid ~2 months of interest on that extra capital — a small cost for the option value of having liquidity during the move and transition period.
Why this beats just putting it down
Putting $150K down at closing vs applying it as a lump sum after saves almost the same total interest, but the FHA route keeps you liquid during the highest-risk period — the first 90 days of homeownership with a newborn and settling-in costs.
The MIP complication
Even after the lump sum dramatically reduces your balance and you may have 20%+ equity, FHA MIP doesn't auto-cancel. You still need to refinance into a conventional loan to remove it. The good news: the lump sum accelerates when you hit that equity threshold.
Refinance trigger
After the lump sum, track your equity closely. Once you hit 20% LTV (accounting for any appreciation), refinance into a conventional loan to drop the MIP. At that point your payment drops and you're on a clean conventional structure. That refi could happen within 1–2 years at this equity level.
What you're giving up
$150K deployed into the house is $150K not invested elsewhere. At a 7% historical average return, that capital could grow to ~$161K in one year. The interest you save on the mortgage has to be weighed against what that cash could earn — especially if your mortgage rate is below your investment return.
The honest verdict
This is arguably the most strategically flexible option in the set. You get FHA's low barrier to entry, preserve liquidity during the highest-risk window, then deploy capital deliberately after closing when you have a clearer picture of your actual cash needs. The main risk is discipline — if the $150K gets spent during the transition period instead of hitting the principal, the strategy collapses.

Lump sum applied at month 3. FHA rate 6.25%. Upfront MIP 1.75% added to balance. Monthly MIP 0.55%. After lump sum, monthly payment remains the same — extra principal payment accelerates payoff without changing your required minimum.

Why this works
Daily balance compression rewards disciplined spenders. Every dollar of surplus parked in the account earns the equivalent of your HELOC rate — currently 8.5% — beating most savings accounts. Lump sums like bonuses immediately reduce interest.
Rate risk is real
HELOCs track prime. Today that's 8.5% — already 2 full points above conventional. If prime rises to 10%, your interest cost climbs with no ceiling or fixed payment to plan around. Variable income plus a child makes this the highest-risk option.
Discipline is the product
This strategy only beats conventional if you spend less than you earn every month. The HELOC uses your home's equity as a credit line — overspending doesn't just drain a bank account, it draws against your house.
Where to find it
True first-lien HELOCs aren't widely available. CMG Financial's "All-In-One" loan is the most common product. Some credit unions offer similar structures. Underwriting tends to be stricter than conventional mortgages.
